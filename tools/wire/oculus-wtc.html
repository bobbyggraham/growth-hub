The Light That Came Back

Twenty-five years after the towers fell, the hall built in their place can hold any color a room full of people needs. This is how it was rebuilt from the inside — and what it asked of the people who wired it.

Written & designed by Bernard J. Erickson
LC, CLEP, CLMC, IES Emeritus · CMO Emeritus / Key Accounts, FSG
Lighting design with Mathle Leyes, LEED AP, LC

The ground

Sixteen acres

There is no way to write about this building without starting where it stands. On September 11, 2001, the ground beneath the World Trade Center became the hardest address in America. Everything that has been built there since carries that.

What went up in the middle of it is not an office tower and not a memorial. It is a train station — a working one, moving a quarter of a million people a day between New Jersey and Lower Manhattan. People pass through it on an ordinary Tuesday on the way to an ordinary job.

That ordinariness is the point. The most human thing that can happen on that ground is a commute. Santiago Calatrava designed a hall that makes the commute feel like something, and then he did one thing more.

The design

A dove from a child’s hand

Calatrava described the form as a dove released from a child’s hand. The Transportation Hub opened to the public in March 2016 after twelve years of construction, and the hall sits deliberately askew — turned off the Manhattan street grid, out of line with every neighbor.

That angle is not an aesthetic choice. It is a clock. The building is oriented so that on one day each year the sun runs the exact axis of the hall. The 335-foot skylight along the spine opens, and a blade of daylight moves down the floor.

It is called the Way of Light, and it is timed to 10:28 a.m. on September 11 — the minute the North Tower came down. Weather permitting, the roof opens and the sun fills the space.

Calatrava called the structure’s supports “columns of light.” He meant it structurally. But it also set the standard that everything we did later had to meet: in this building, light is not a finish. It is the architecture.

The brief

What the owner asked for

The original lighting design was developed long before LED could deliver that intent. It was built with ceramic metal halide because, at the time, nothing else could throw that far and hold that quality.

Then the space did what great spaces do: it changed jobs. Alongside the trains and the retail, the Oculus became one of the most requested rooms in New York for receptions and social functions — and the people booking it kept asking for the same thing. Color.

By then solid-state lighting had caught up. The owner wanted four things at once: keep the original design intent exactly, introduce color with light, cut the energy, and make the system something a facilities team could actually live with.

The problem

Why it was hard

Every constraint on this job came from the same place: nothing about the building was allowed to change. Not the fixture locations, not the wiring, not the light on the ribs, not the dignity of the room.

01

RGBW in almost no space

The retrofit had to live inside the existing enclosures, at the existing locations, with no significant re-wiring — while pushing enough optical power to graze ribs that climb hundreds of feet. Four emitters had to fit where one metal halide lamp used to sit.

02

DMX with real security

Color control means a network, and a network at this address means a hardened one. The control design had to give the owner full DMX scene control without opening a door that does not belong in this building.

03

Installing in a sacred public place

The hall never stopped being a transit hub and never stopped being a memorial. Lifts, crews and schedules had to work around commuters, around events, and around the days when this room means more than a room.

04

Consensus at a landmark

A building like this has many stewards — the owner, the operator, the original design team, the public. Getting to one specification meant getting every one of them to yes, in writing, before a single fixture moved.

The result

166 columns, one specification

Custom PAR38-scale RGBW optical arrays, built as retrofit kits to drop into the existing housings, running on an ETC Paradigm architectural control system with touchscreen control of the scenes.

The distribution held. The levels held. The design intent held. What changed is that the owner can now put the room in any color the occasion calls for — a holiday, a cause, a nation’s flag — from a touchscreen, in seconds, without a single truss or temporary rig.

And the building spends less than a fifth of the energy it used to spend doing it. In a space this size, run this many hours, that is not a rounding error. That is the kind of number a facilities director takes to a board.

Recognized by the industry

The Illuminating Engineering Society named Transforming The Oculus a 2021 Illumination Award of Merit for Interior Lighting Design, presented to Bernard J. Erickson and Mathle Leyes — in recognition of meritorious contribution to lighting design.

The work was also presented as an AIA-registered continuing-education course through the Designers Lighting Forum.

Timeless contribution and service

FSG’s vision is to be an institution dedicated to timeless contribution and service. This project is what that sentence looks like when it is built.

Timeless was the discipline to change everything about how the light is made and nothing about how the room feels — to serve someone else’s design intent with more care than if it had been our own. Contribution was solving a problem that had no off-the-shelf answer, in a space where being close enough was not an option. Service was the part nobody photographs: the lifts at odd hours, the consensus meetings, the security reviews, the handover to a team who has to run it for the next twenty years.

We do not get to decide which buildings matter most to people. We only get to decide how we show up at them. On this one, the work has to be right on the eleventh of September at 10:28 in the morning, and every other minute of the year.
