Show Up & Run the Room

The FSG meeting play for every room — in person, on camera, or on the trade show floor. Open with the win, lead by directive, and never leave without a date on the calendar.
Build your next meeting Plan it, run Deal Coach, and paste the invite into your calendar in five minutes.
Sales Leadership  ·  November 2026  ·  Development Play

Why This Play Exists

Most meetings die in the first three minutes and never recover. Nobody states the point, the seller talks for twenty minutes, and everyone leaves with a “let’s circle back.” Nothing moves.

This play sets one standard for every FSG meeting — customer, partner, or internal; one person, a conference room, or a full audience: open with the result, run the clock, and close with a clear call to action.

The Standard

If you can’t say in one sentence what they walk away with, you’re not ready to send the invite.

01Show Up Ready

Showing up starts the day before. Ten minutes of prep is the difference between running the room and reacting to it. It starts with one sentence: “By the end of this meeting, we will ___.” Most meetings are built around one of three things:

A problem to solve

“By the end of this meeting, we’ll know the three stores with the most repeat lighting calls and agree on which one we price first.”

A project to move

“By the end of this meeting, we’ll lock the Phase 2 scope, crew schedule, and a start date your store managers can plan around.”

Results to act on

“By the end of this meeting, you’ll see the 18% energy savings from Phase 1 and decide whether we roll it out to the other 22 sites.”

  • Write your one-sentence result — a problem, a project, or results.
  • Send the agenda with the invite — three items max, each with a time box.
  • Know the account: open opportunities in Engage, last touch, who’s in the room and what each person owns.
  • Prepare five fact-finding questions you don’t already know the answer to.
  • Decide the ask you’ll make at the close — before you show up, not during.
  • Arrive or log in early. Camera on, phone face down, notes open.
  • Bringing a team? Assign roles: who leads, who takes notes, who owns the technical answers.

02Stand and Deliver

The play doesn’t change by room — the win, the clock, and the ask work everywhere. Your delivery does. Start with the basics that hold in every setting, then pick your setting and your audience.

Plant your feet

Standing or seated, set your base and stay still. Movement should mean something — a transition, not nerves.

Land the sentence

Finish every sentence on one person’s eyes. Drop your voice at the end — rising tones sound like questions.

Own the pause

Pause after the win, after a key number, and after the ask. Silence reads as confidence. “Um” reads as the opposite.

Hands visible

Open hands, used at chest height to count or frame a point. No pockets, no crossed arms, no pen clicking.

Energy +10%

A notch above how you feel. It reads as normal to the room — and on camera, go higher.

Say their name

Use it in the first minute and again at the ask. It’s the fastest way to make one person feel like the only person.

Pick your setting

You have the whole room to work with. Use the space on purpose.

Before they arrive
  • Get there 10 minutes early and see the room.
  • Pick a seat where you can see every face — the corner of the table, not across it.
  • Phone out of sight, leave-behind ready, one copy per person.
Body
  • Standing: feet shoulder-width, weight even, shoulders back.
  • Seated: lean in slightly, both feet down, hands on the table.
  • Firm handshake, eye contact, their name back to them.
Voice and eyes
  • Talk to the person, not the slide or the screen behind you.
  • Match their pace. If they’re clipped, be crisp. If they’re relaxed, slow down.
Watch out for
  • Sitting before the host offers you a seat.
  • Checking your watch or phone — even once.
  • Spreading paper across their desk.

The camera flattens everything. You have to bring more to show the same.

Before you join
  • Camera at eye level, light in front of you, clean background.
  • Test the link and screen share. Close email and notifications.
  • Join two minutes early so you’re there to greet them.
Body
  • Frame head and shoulders with room for your hands to gesture.
  • Sit tall, or stand if you can — your voice gets stronger.
  • Smile in the first five seconds. It sets the tone of the whole call.
Voice and eyes
  • Look at the lens when you state the win and make the ask — that’s eye contact for them.
  • Call on people by name. There’s no body language to read who wants to talk.
  • Wait two extra seconds after a question. Unmuting takes time.
  • Drop the ask and the next-step link in the chat at the close.
Watch out for
  • Talking to the grid of faces instead of the lens.
  • Reading notes off to the side — they can see your eyes move.
  • Sharing your whole desktop.

You have two minutes and a crowded aisle. The goal isn’t to sell — it’s to earn the next meeting.

Position
  • Stand at the front edge of the booth, facing the aisle — never behind the table.
  • No sitting, eating, or phones in the booth. No huddling with your own team.
Open in ten seconds
  • Lead with a question, not a pitch: “What’s the one facilities project on your plate for next year?”
  • Read the badge, use their name, and ask about their company — not yours.
Qualify fast
  • Three things: their role, a real project, and when it happens.
  • No project in the next 12 months? Be kind, be brief, and move on.
Watch out for
  • “Can I help you?” — it gets “just looking” every time.
  • Handing out a brochure as the close.
  • Pitching the whole line card to someone who doesn’t qualify.

Pick your audience

It’s a conversation, not a presentation. They should talk 70% of the time.

Deliver
  • Sit at an angle, not head-on — it feels like working together.
  • Mirror their energy and pace.
  • Use their name two or three times, no more.
The ask
  • One person can say yes — so ask them directly and wait for the answer.
  • If they can’t say yes, ask who can and how you get in front of them.

You’re running the room now. Know who’s in it before you start.

Deliver
  • Find the decision-maker, the champion, and the skeptic before the meeting.
  • Run intros by why each person is here — and ask theirs what they want out of today.
  • Ask each role a question from their stakeholder card.
  • Spread your eye contact — don’t play only to the most senior person.
  • Call on quiet people by name. Park side debates.
The ask
  • Get the group to agree out loud on the next step.
  • Assign owners by name, with dates, before anyone leaves.

You’re presenting to a room — a lunch-and-learn, a panel, a keynote, an Engage Live. Lead with energy and one idea.

Open
  • Open with a story or a number, never your bio. Memorize the first line cold.
  • Tell them the win in the first 30 seconds: what they’ll leave with.
Deliver
  • Stand still to land a point. Move only on transitions.
  • One person per thought — left, center, right sections of the room.
  • Three points, slow down 20%, and pause after every key line.
Q&A
  • Repeat the question so the whole room hears it.
  • Answer to everyone, not just the person who asked.
  • Never end on Q&A — end on your ask.
Watch out for
  • Reading slides with your back to the room.
  • Pacing, swaying, or apologizing for running long.
  • An ask with five options. Give them one: scan, book, or visit.

03The Meeting Killers

These openers and habits tell the room you don’t have a plan. Every one of them is fixable before the meeting starts. Tap a card to see what the room hears and what to do instead.

04The Banned Phrase List

Corporate slang fills air without moving anything forward. Customers have heard every one of these a thousand times — and they’ve learned none of them mean anything. Tap a card to see what it really says and what to say instead.

Rule of thumb: if a phrase could show up in any company’s meeting about any topic, cut it. Replace it with a number, a name, or a date. Deal Coach catches these in your invite.

05Talk Less, Find More

The seller who talks the most usually learns the least — and sells the least. Every minute you spend pitching is a minute you’re not learning what the customer would actually pay to fix. Target 30% you, 70% them.

Signs you’ve fallen into the trap

  • You’re ten minutes in and haven’t asked a question.
  • You answer questions nobody asked: “And another thing our team does…”
  • You fill every silence. Silence is where the customer thinks — let it work.
  • You walk the full capability deck before you know the problem.
  • You ask yes/no questions and get one-word answers.
  • You know they had a good weekend — but they know your weekend, your kids, and your flight.
  • You leave without knowing the budget, the decision-maker, or the deadline.

Questions that work in any room

The real problem

“What’s the one facilities issue that keeps landing on your desk?”

Cost of doing nothing

“What happens if this isn’t fixed by next summer?”

Priority

“Where does this rank against everything else on your plate this year?”

How they decide

“Walk me through how a project like this gets approved — who else weighs in?”

Budget

“Where does funding stand — this year’s budget, or building the case for next year?”

Timeline

“What date would make this a win for you?”

Past experience

“What did your last vendor do that you never want to deal with again?”

Success

“Twelve months from now, how will you know this was the right call?”

Know who’s in the room

Every stakeholder owns something different and loses sleep over something different. Ask about their job, not your scope — the right question to the wrong role gets you a polite non-answer.

Facility Manager

Site level · lives in the work orders

Owns

Day-to-day uptime, work orders, and every vendor who shows up on site.

Problems they face
  • Repeat calls on aging equipment
  • Thin staff covering too many buildings
  • Vendors who no-show or leave a mess
Ask
  • “What’s the work order that keeps coming back?”
  • “Which sites call you after hours the most?”
  • “When a vendor misses, what does it cost you?”

Director / VP of Facilities

Portfolio level · owns the plan

Owns

The portfolio, the capital plan, vendor strategy, and reporting up to leadership.

Problems they face
  • A deferred-maintenance backlog bigger than the budget
  • Inconsistent vendors region to region
  • Proving facilities spend is an investment
Ask
  • “What’s on next year’s capital plan that isn’t funded yet?”
  • “How many vendors are you managing across the portfolio today?”
  • “What would you need to show your boss to call this a win?”

Procurement / Sourcing

Owns the contract · protects the company

Owns

Vendor selection, contract terms, pricing structure, and vendor risk.

Problems they face
  • Bids that can’t be compared apples to apples
  • Pressure to consolidate spend
  • Vendor insurance and compliance gaps
Ask
  • “How do you weigh a national vendor against a local one?”
  • “What pricing structure makes your job easiest — unit, T&M, or fixed?”
  • “Once a vendor is picked, what’s the path and timeline to a signed contract?”

CFO / Finance

Owns the money · thinks in payback

Owns

The budget, capex versus opex, ROI hurdles, and spend predictability.

Problems they face
  • Surprise repair spend that wrecks the forecast
  • Energy cost volatility
  • Projects that can’t show payback
Ask
  • “What payback period gets a project approved here?”
  • “Does this land as capex or opex in your world?”
  • “Which facilities line item surprised you most this year?”

Energy / Sustainability Lead

Owns the targets · needs the data

Owns

Energy spend, ESG and carbon targets, rebates, and the reporting behind them.

Problems they face
  • Public targets with real deadlines
  • Gaps in site-level energy data
  • Rebates that expire before projects start
Ask
  • “What’s your reduction target, and by when?”
  • “Where does your energy data come from today?”
  • “Which rebates or incentives are you chasing this year?”

IFM / Property Manager

JLL, CBRE, Cushman · answers to their client

Owns

The client relationship, SLAs, vendor performance, and closeout paperwork.

Problems they face
  • SLA pressure and client escalations
  • Vendors who miss documentation and photos
  • Managing too many subs to one standard
Ask
  • “Which of your client’s KPIs is under the most pressure?”
  • “What does a vendor do that makes you look good to your client?”
  • “How do you want work orders, photos, and closeouts delivered?”

Operations / Regional Leader

Owns the business running inside the building

Owns

Store or branch uptime, customer experience, brand standards, and their people.

Problems they face
  • Installs that disrupt customers or staff
  • After-hours access and security
  • Inconsistent look site to site
Ask
  • “When can we work without hurting your business — nights, weekends?”
  • “What does a bad install day look like for your team?”
  • “Who on site needs to sign off when we finish?”

EHS / Safety

Owns compliance · can stop a job

Owns

OSHA and NFPA 70E compliance, contractor safety, incidents, and audits.

Problems they face
  • Arc flash studies past due
  • Contractor prequal and site safety
  • Open findings from the last audit
Ask
  • “When was your last arc flash study done?”
  • “What does your contractor safety prequal look like?”
  • “What finding from your last audit is still open?”

Three habits that change everything

  1. Ask, then stop. Count to three in your head before you speak again.
  2. Go one layer deeper. “Tell me more about that.” The second answer is where the truth lives.
  3. Play it back. “So what I’m hearing is ___ — did I get that right?” It proves you listened and earns you the right to propose.

06Open With the Win

The first 60 seconds decide whether people lean in or check email. Earn a little trust, open with the result they’ll leave with, then take control of the clock.

Small talk: about them, on purpose

Rapport matters — people buy from people they trust. But small talk isn’t a warm-up act for your stories. Keep it short, keep it about them, and use it to learn. Every personal detail they share is something you can bring back next time.

Do
  • Keep it to 60–90 seconds, then bridge: “I want to respect your time — here’s what you’ll leave with today.”
  • Open with something specific to them: the new building, a recent win, the photo on the wall, the show they just walked.
  • Follow up twice: “How did that come together?” “What’s next for it?” The second answer is where you learn something.
  • If they ask about you, answer in one true sentence — then hand it back. “Good — busy season. How was the trip to Austin?”
  • Write down what they share and log it in Engage. Asking about their daughter’s tournament next time is how trust compounds.
Don’t
  • Tell your weekend story, your flight story, or your kids’ stats.
  • Refuse to share anything — zero back makes it feel like an interrogation. One sentence, then back to them.
  • Go near politics, religion, gossip, or complaining. Ever.
  • Force it. If they open with “What do you have for me?” — skip the small talk and lead with the win.

In a group, it’s one line about the room. On camera, use the minute while people join — talk to whoever is there first. At a booth, their badge is your opener.

Introductions: why they’re in the room

Name-and-title round robins burn minutes and teach you nothing — titles are already in the invite. What matters is why each person is here. The leader runs introductions; nobody gives a bio.

RoomHow to run it
1-on-1No introductions. Rapport, then the win.
Small group (3–6)You introduce your side in one pass, each person tied to their problem. Then each of them shares their role and the one thing they want out of today.
Larger group (7–12)You introduce your side. Ask their lead to introduce theirs — or skip names and ask the room: “What has to happen today for this to be worth your time?”
Audience / stageNo round robin. Name the key people in the room, and use a show of hands or a chat question to learn who’s there.
Talk Track — Introducing Your Side “Quick intros, then we’ll dive in. Maria runs our estimating — she’s here so any pricing question gets a real answer today, not a follow-up. Tom leads our field crews in Florida — he’s the one who’ll be standing on your sites. Now, I’d love to hear from each of you: your role, and the one thing you want out of the next 30 minutes.”
Why it works
  • “Why you’re here” tells the customer what each person can do for them.
  • “What you want out of today” is discovery before discovery — you hear each stakeholder’s priority in their own words.
  • Write down every answer. Play them back at the close: “Dana wanted a budget number, Chris wanted a start date — here’s both.”
Watch out for
  • “Who wants to go next?” — call on people by name, in order.
  • Your own team giving bios and tenure. Fifteen seconds each, max.
  • Bringing someone who doesn’t have a reason to be there. If you can’t say why, leave them off the invite.
  • On camera: put names and roles in the invite so nobody spends the call reading them out.

The open, step by step

Get the win on the table inside 90 seconds. Rapport first, then the play — intros come after, so every person ties to the win.

  1. Rapport — about them, 60 seconds. One specific question, one follow-up, then bridge.
  2. Call the play — state the win. “Thanks for the 30 minutes. By the end of today, you’ll have a clear plan and a budget number to retrofit your top 10 sites before Q2.”
  3. Say why it matters to them. “That gets you ahead of the rebate deadline and takes lighting complaints off your plate.”
  4. Introductions — groups only. Your side by why they’re here (“Maria’s here so pricing gets answered today”); their side by the one thing they want out of today.
  5. Preview the agenda. “Three things: what’s driving this, what we’ve seen work, and the next step.”
  6. Hand them the floor. “Before I go further — what would make this meeting a home run for you?”
Talk Track — Customer Meeting “Thanks for making time, Dana. In the next 30 minutes my goal is to leave you with a clear path to cut lighting maintenance calls across your 40 stores. I’ll ask a few questions about what’s happening today, share how we’ve handled this for similar portfolios, and we’ll agree on whether a site walk makes sense. Anything you’d add?”
Talk Track — Internal Team Meeting “We’re here to make one decision: which three accounts get the Q1 pursuit team. Twenty minutes. Marcus walks the data, we debate for ten, and we leave with names and owners.”

07Run the Clock

Lead by directive. You own the agenda, the time, and the transitions — polite, but in charge. Here’s how a 30-minute customer meeting breaks down. Protect the last four minutes: that’s where the deal moves.

Open0–3
Discovery — they talk3–20 min
Fit20–26
Close26–30

Open: a minute on them, then the win, why it matters, the agenda. Discovery: questions, follow-ups, play it back. Fit: two or three proof points tied to what you just heard — nothing else. Close: recap, the ask, owners, dates.

Directive transitions

  • “That’s helpful. Let’s shift to how projects get approved.”
  • “I want to protect your time — let’s park that and come back if we have room.”
  • “We’ve got five minutes left, so let’s lock in next steps.”

    08Close With a Call to Action

    No meeting ends without a decision, an owner, and a date. If the next step isn’t on a calendar before you hang up, it isn’t real.
